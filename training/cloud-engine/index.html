The Cloud Engine

Inland flying runs on an invisible engine. This is how to tell if it will fire — and how high it will take you.

Let's go ↓
The big idea

A thermal is just a hot-air balloon with no basket

On an inland day, the sun heats the ground, the ground heats the air, and blobs of that warm air peel off and rise. Those rising blobs — thermals — are what we climb in. The whole game is reading whether the engine will start, and how far up it will run before it hits a ceiling.

Everything below builds one picture, one step at a time. No maths. Just a bubble, a lid, and a cloud.

Meet the three players

Every thermal day is these three things fighting it out

🫧

The Bubble

Warm air leaving the ground. The hotter the ground, the warmer and more buoyant the bubble — and the harder it pushes up.

🚧

The Lid

A warm, stable layer partway up (an inversion). It acts like a ceiling that weak bubbles simply can't get through.

☁️

The Cloud

When a bubble cools to its dew point it turns to cloud. Making cloud releases heat — a turbo-boost that helps punch through the lid.

The tool

What you're looking at in the app

The SkewT in the SkyHigh app draws the same three players as lines. You don't need to read it like a scientist — just know which line is which.

  • 🌡️
    Red line — the temperature of the air around you, at every height.
  • 💧
    Green line — the moisture (dew point). How far it sits from red tells you how dry the air is.
  • 🫧
    Orange dashed — your bubble rising. This is the thermal.
  • 🔶
    The orange dot at the bottom is the ground temperature. Drag it to ask "what if the ground gets this hot?"
Golden rule: the bubble keeps rising for as long as the orange line stays warmer than (to the right of) the red line. Where orange crosses back over red, the thermal stops — that height is your thermal top.

The real thing · a plot twist

One real day, four drags of the orange dot

Here's an actual inland sounding. As we heat the ground a single degree at a time, the answer swings wildly — including one result that feels completely backwards.

1Cold: stuck under the lid

Top ≈ 3,400 ft

Ground barely warm. The bubble is feeble, bumps into the lid and stops. Cloudbase is right on the deck.

2+1°: the day switches ON

Top ≈ 10,900 ft

One extra degree is just enough to punch through the lid. Above it the air is unstable, so the bubble keeps accelerating — 3,400 → nearly 11,000 ft from a single degree.

Why so sudden? It's a tipping point, like a cork held under a shelf underwater. Push gently — nothing. A hair harder and it slips past the edge and shoots to the surface. Breaking an inversion is exactly that: nothing, nothing, nothing… then whoosh.

3Warmer still: it COLLAPSES 🤯

Top ≈ 2,500 ft

Hotter ground gave a lower top?! Heating the ground adds no moisture, so cloudbase climbs. Now the bubble is dry all the way to the lid, cools fast, and quits before it can make cloud and get the turbo. Top crashes down onto a rising cloudbase.

The damp-match analogy: the low-cloudbase bubble is a match that catches instantly and lights the whole bonfire (11,000 ft). The high-cloudbase bubble is a damp match — by the time it would finally light, the thermal has already fizzled out against the lid.

4Baking hot: blasts through

Top ≈ 21,000 ft

Crank the ground to unrealistically hot and the bubble is so over-buoyant it ignores the lid entirely, climbs ~10,000 ft dry to a very high cloudbase, then rides the cloud-turbo into the flight levels.

The whole story on one line

Cloudbase rises smoothly. The top goes wild.

As you heat the ground, cloudbase climbs steadily and predictably — it's just dew-point maths. But the thermal top jumps, collapses, then soars, because of its fight with the lid.

Flying it for real

What a pilot actually takes from this

  • 🎯
    A giant jump means a knife-edge day. If a single degree swings the top from 3,000 to 11,000 ft, the real day could land anywhere in there. Treat the exact number with suspicion.
  • ☁️
    Low cloudbase below the lid = the good stuff. That's when thermals light up early and punch high. A cloudbase that has climbed above the lid can mean a capped, disappointing day.
  • 🌬️
    Always check the wind up high. A towering top is no use if it's blowing 35 kt up there — read the wind barbs before you get excited.
  • ⚡
    CAPE is a storm flag, not a thermal-strength meter. High CAPE with these deep tops is your cue to watch for overdevelopment, not to celebrate.
  • 🧠
    The tool shows potential, not a promise. Real bubbles mix in dry air and never reach the textbook top. Use it to understand the day — then make your own call.
